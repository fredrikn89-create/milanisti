Perspective · Sporting management

Published Tuesday 20 October 2026

By Alexandra

A job title is not a mandate

Milan are to get a Head of Football, and everything points to it being Markus Krösche. What matters is not whether he is good, but what he is actually allowed to decide.

It is easy to feel relief when a name finally appears. For months no one on the outside has been able to point to who ultimately answers for the sporting side of Milan, and in that situation a job title like Head of Football looks like a cold drink on a hot day. It is reported that Markus Krösche, who has now left Eintracht Frankfurt, is close to a deal that could be in place within the week, against compensation of a few million euros to the German club. Milan have not confirmed anything, and I am therefore writing about what is expected, not about what has happened.

But a job title is not a mandate, and it is the mandate that decides whether the hire becomes a turning point or just another line on the org chart. The post is described as something along the lines of what Maldini had, but without the formal sporting-director qualification. At the same time it is unclear what authority Krösche will get, and how he will fit in among those already at the club. It is my view that this is the only question that matters. A leader who does not control the transfer windows, the squad budget and the line to the owner is a spokesman on better pay, and Milan have had plenty of voices that explain and too few that decide.

Then there is the profile. Krösche's reputation is built on finding players others overlooked, buying them cheaply and selling them dearly. Kolo Muani and Marmoush, who were both signed without a transfer fee and sold on for large sums, are the two examples that always get cited. It is a valuable skill for a club that cannot or will not spend without limits, and it is probably why Cardinale wanted him as early as the summer. But the assignment being described is the opposite of what he has done so far: to build a team that can compete in Serie A and the Champions League, not an asset that can be realised. Selling at the top and building something that lasts are two different crafts, and we do not yet know which of them Milan have ordered.

The order of events says something too. Amorim was brought in in June, long before the person who will be responsible for football was in place, and Krösche is said to have viewed the coaching choice positively. That is reassuring on the surface and a little crooked beneath it: a Head of Football who takes over a coach and a squad he did not choose does not get to set the conditions but is judged on the results. The first real test of whether he can shape and not just inherit will be the January transfer window.

Besides, Milan will reportedly have to find a separate sporting director alongside him. That is not necessarily wrong, since many well-run clubs split the work this way, but it forces the club to say out loud what it has not said out loud so far: who picks the players, who negotiates, and who finally has the conversation with the coach when something is not working.

I want this to go well. Krösche is a serious, results-driven director with a reputation for understanding the market better than most, and that is exactly what Milan need. But I have seen enough clubs hire a capable man for a post with no substance to know how it ends. Milan have not needed a new job title in the past five months. Milan have needed someone who answers when things go wrong, and if Krösche is to be that person, someone has to write it down before he signs.

Eintracht Frankfurt sporting director Markus Krösche was released from his duties on Sunday at his own request. On Monday Matteo Moretto reports that he is "a un passo" (practically there) from becoming Milan's new Head of Football. Milan have confirmed nothing.

His contract, which runs to 2028, remains in place, but on reduced terms, and Axel Hellmann takes over responsibility for sport on an interim basis. Eintracht expect a move could bring several million euros in compensation.

Krösche is known in Germany as a director who has made a lot of money buying and selling players, including Kolo Muani and Marmoush. A new head of sporting operations will shape how Milan build the squad going forward.

Match report · Serie A · round 7

Published Sunday 18 October 2026

By Gianni

Milan led 3–0 and sweated their way past Atalanta

Milan played a good first hour and looked like a team heading for an easy evening, before the match suddenly turned nervy. Three points and a brilliant Rabiot are what matter most, but the closing stages have become a pattern Amorim has to solve, because Milan cannot keep letting a three-goal lead turn into a shaky win.

Milan beat Atalanta 3–2 at San Siro on Sunday after leading 3–0, and Atalanta came back with two goals in fifteen minutes. Ramos (penalty, 24th minute), Rabiot (43rd) and Gila (57th) scored for Milan, Gaetano (66th) and Krstović (81st) for Atalanta. Milan have 15 points after four wins and three draws in seven rounds.

Amorim set up in the expected 3–4–2–1: Maignan behind Gila, De Winter and Pavlović, with Chukwueze, Musah, Modrić and Moreira in midfield and Rabiot and Pulisic behind Ramos. Atalanta lined up in a 4–2–3–1 under Maurizio Sarri. It rained in Milan, and Chiffi was the referee. Pulisic had a goal disallowed for a narrow offside early on, and in the 20th minute Kolašinac had to go off with a muscle problem for Atalanta.

It was substitute Obrić who gave Milan the lead: he stopped a shot from Musah with his arm, and after a review Chiffi pointed to the spot. Ramos sent Carnesecchi the wrong way and made it 1–0 in the 24th minute. Krstović got past Maignan in a breakaway at the other end, but shot wide.

Just before the break it was 2–0. After a combination between Modrić, Moreira and Rabiot, Rabiot finished low to the far post in the 43rd minute, two minutes after he had been booked for protesting. Early in the second half, after Hutchinson had come on for Chukwueze in the 53rd minute, Milan got another penalty when Kristensen blocked a shot with his hand. Ramos changed corner this time, but Carnesecchi saved. From the corner that followed, Modrić crossed, Rabiot flicked on, and Gila headed in 3–0 at the far post in the 57th minute.

Then the game turned. In the 66th minute Zalewski controlled a deflected long shot and played the ball to Gaetano, who hammered in 3–1, and Krstović hit the post around the same time. Around the 75th minute Amorim took off three players and sent on Estupiñán, Jashari and Loftus-Cheek. In the 81st minute Bellanova pulled back a low ball, and Krstović made it 3–2. Zappacosta had a big chance for 3–3 in the 83rd minute, but shot straight at Maignan, and Milan held on, with Tomori on for Gila from the 84th minute and five minutes of added time.

Amorim was not happy with the closing stages. “At the end of the day, the result is the most important thing in football,” he said, “but the performance must also be analysed and discussed: we risked scoring the fourth goal.” On why the game turned: “We stopped pressing and became nervous. We have to suffer less.” He felt both goals conceded were avoidable: “The two goals could have been avoided,” and pointed out that midfielders running into the box have to be tracked better. On Moreira, who had a lot of defensive work against Atalanta's quick winger: “He played well at least until the 66th minute.” The final minutes still do not change his view of the progress: Milan are getting better.

On Rabiot, who had a yellow card from before the break, Amorim said he had spoken to him at half-time: “If I had thought he could get a red card, I would have taken him off, but I felt everything was under control.” Rabiot pointed to the mind himself: “It's not physical, it's mental. We were three goals up, and then we lowered our attention and concentration.” He believes those who come on from the bench must do more, and took up the referee: “There were 34 fouls against us and not a single yellow card. Far too often there are wrong decisions, and we have to say so.” At the same time he admitted that he himself must stop going to talk to the referee.

Gila, who scored from the corner, saw it like this: “We started the game well, then we fell apart in the second half. But the most important thing is to win.” He said he is very happy to be at Milan and called the team amazing. Ramos did not want to dwell on the penalties: “There is no time to think about it.” On the saved penalty, he said he had tried to surprise the goalkeeper. The coach has asked him to be in the box more, and the goal is simple: “We want to win every match.” Assessment: Milan did the job over a good first hour, but 3–0 turned into a match that was not settled until the final whistle, and the two goals conceded resemble what Amorim himself points to: a lack of pressing and control in transitions. Bournemouth in the Europa League await on Thursday.

Perspective · Amorim

Published Friday 16 October 2026

By Alexandra

The manager who said no thanks to the praise

Milan won 4–0 in Salzburg, and after the match it was Rúben Amorim who asked to turn the celebrations down. That says more about this season than the hat-trick itself.

It is easy to get carried away by a 4–0 against a team that had not lost in fifteen matches, which is exactly why the moment after the final whistle is so telling. When Sky suggested this was Milan's most complete performance, Amorim replied that he did not agree. The team had played better in other matches, he said, and the result flattered the picture: Milan lost the ball too close to their own box, had problems building up, and a more precise Salzburg could have scored.

Answers like that are rarer than they should be. Most managers take the praise, put it in a frame and let it hang until the next defeat. Amorim does the opposite, and it is hard to read it as anything but a deliberate choice: he does not want his players to think the yardstick was moved by one good night in Austria. That is my assessment, but it fits with the rest of what he said.

The night was not only about winning, either. Milan had lost 0–2 at home to Benfica in the opening round, and Amorim still fielded Tomori in his first start of the season and Camarda, Bartesaghi, Jashari, Hutchinson and Cissé from the first minute. He admitted the risk himself: had it gone wrong, he knew exactly what criticism would have followed. This is a manager who is willing to risk being pilloried, and honest enough to say so out loud.

Cissé is the obvious headline. Three goals, one with his head and two with his feet, in under fifty minutes turned Milan's attack from a question into an opportunity. Amorim called the hat-trick extraordinary and credited his explosiveness, but added at once that he must improve, in training as well. Cissé himself waved the praise aside, saying the goals were secondary to the win. He came from Serie B, he said, and his teammates have made the move easier. Five goals so far this season from a player new to the Milan shirt is a good argument for patience.

Camarda is the more interesting case, precisely because the answer about him was less flattering. Amorim praised his reading of the game and his movement in the box, but also said he lacks explosiveness, pace and rhythm, and that this was true against Salzburg as well. It is the kind of criticism you only aim at a player you want a future with. That the staff are at the same time working on getting him to last a full match says what the club actually sees in him.

Yet this is where the contradiction sits. Amorim says he needs twenty-two competitive players, that nobody can feel safe, and that he cannot keep the same eleven for the next few matches. That is the language of rotation, and it sounds right in a season of Thursdays and Sundays in quick succession. But rotation is also a test, because it only works if the team does not stop functioning when half the eleven changes. Salzburg shows that it can work. It does not prove that it works every time.

That is why I like the cold shower more than the result itself. A team that believes it played its best when it really won on efficiency tends to forget what did not work, and what did not work was the build-up and the balls lost near its own box. Amorim has pointed it out himself, which means the problem has been identified before it costs points.

Atalanta come on Sunday, and then we will see whether the message was heard. Praise is rarely what makes a team better, and a manager who waves it away himself has at least shown that he knows the difference between a good night and a good performance.

Match report · Europa League · round 2

Published Thursday 15 October 2026

By Gianni

Cissé hat-trick as Milan crush Salzburg

Milan beat Salzburg 4–0 away, with Alphadjo Cissé scoring three times. It gave Milan their first points of this season's Europa League, after the 2–0 defeat to Benfica in the opening round.

Milan beat Salzburg 4–0 in the Europa League on Thursday evening, and Alphadjo Cissé stole the show with three goals. It was Milan's first points in the competition this season, and Salzburg still have not beaten Milan in five meetings.

Rúben Amorim rotated, as he had said he would, and on paper lined up with three at the back: Tomori, De Winter and Pavlović in front of Maignan, with Hutchinson and Bartesaghi as wing-backs, Rabiot and Jashari in the centre and Loftus-Cheek and Cissé behind Camarda. In play the structure became a hybrid 4–2–3–1. Milan led 2–0 at the break.

Milan led after a quarter of an hour. Cissé finished with his left foot from the middle of the box in the 15th minute after Hutchinson's cross, and in the 32nd he got his head to a cross from Bartesaghi to make it 2–0.

Four minutes into the second half it was over. Loftus-Cheek started a counter-attack, Rabiot set it up, and Cissé only had to tap the ball in for his hat-trick in the 49th minute. They were his first three goals in European competition, all from close range.

Amorim made his changes in the 60th minute: Modrić on for Rabiot, Pulisic for Cissé and Ramos for Camarda. Twelve minutes later Ramos got his. Jashari pressed, Mazurek lost the ball, and the Portuguese was one-on-one with the keeper. After a lot of criticism it was a goal he needed.

Tomori went off in the 67th minute for Filippo Terracciano, and in the 81st Sankhoun Diawara made his Milan debut as a replacement for Pavlović. In the 83rd minute Terracciano was sent off for fouling Schmid as the last man, and Milan played out the match with ten men and kept a clean sheet. Maignan made only three saves.

Amorim was not happy with everything, and he said so to Sky Sport. “I have seen better performances collectively, but everyone only looks at the scoreline,” he said, adding: “Today we gave away a bit too much. If they had been more clinical in front of goal, they would have punished us.” On Cissé he was of two minds: “He has exceptional physical strength and is really devastating in space,” but “he still has to sort out several details, starting with his attitude in training”. On the rotation he said it was unthinkable to field the same team with all the matches ahead, and that nights like this “raise the confidence of those who play less”.

At the press conference after the match Amorim was more personal. On Cissé he said it was not his idea: “It is not my intuition, because the club bought Cissé before I arrived,” and that the club “saw the same thing I saw”. The praise was measured: “He has had better games, but he was good in the box,” “he owes me nothing, he is taking his space” and “he has a lot of heart, but can do better in training and in matches”. On Camarda he said he “reads matches well”, but must improve in explosiveness, pace and rhythm. Hutchinson, who played on the right, “can play there, and he can play as a number 10. It is important to have more options”. On the criticism aimed at himself he said: “It is not about me. I know it is difficult around the club. I chose the two hardest clubs I could choose in my last two jobs. We are at the start of the season.” He added that the rotation was a risk, and that from tomorrow the team must begin preparing for Atalanta.

Amorim himself felt Milan had shown more control in other matches, and pointed to balls lost in build-up and to Salzburg being able to punish them. Maignan made only three saves. According to Amorim the rotation gave good answers, Camarda got his chance without scoring, and now Atalanta await at San Siro on Sunday at 18.00.

Those were the years when people began to speak of greatness in the past tense.

Then came Pioli, and something fell into place. For four and a half years Milan had a manager with time, a project one could recognise and a team that played as if it knew what it was. The results followed: the first league title since 2011, a Champions League semi-final and a second-place finish in his final season. It was not perfect, but it held together, and coherence is precisely what Milan has lacked since.

After Pioli, the managerial carousel started spinning again. Amorim is the fourth new manager in two years, and the Europa League is no accident. It is the bill.

The Benfica match was a reminder of what such a bill looks like in practice: 60 per cent of the ball, 17 shots, only four on target and a defeat that was hard to argue with. Amorim has twelve points after six league rounds and is unbeaten, but three draws in six matches, two of them after leading, make the numbers look better than the quality. And a manager who takes out five regular starters for a home game has already said something about what he thinks of his own team.

Then comes the question I cannot quite put down: are we convinced that Amorim is the man to bring stability and greatness back to Milan? His contract runs to 2029, and Cardinale has praised him as one of the most innovative coaches of his generation. But he was sacked by Manchester United in January after 25 wins in 63 matches, and I still miss a Milan that resembles him, a team in which one can see where it is heading, not merely that it is trying.

Salzburg on Thursday, away, against a side unbeaten in fifteen matches under Danny Röhl, is a poor occasion to start explaining. It is, however, a good occasion to start convincing. Patience is not the same as trust, and Milan has shown that trust can take years to build and a single season to lose.
